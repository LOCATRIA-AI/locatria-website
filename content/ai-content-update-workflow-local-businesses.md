# AI Content Update Workflow for Local Businesses

Most local businesses that publish content do a good job of creating it once and a poor job of maintaining it afterward. An article gets written, published, maybe shared once, and then left alone — sometimes for years. Meanwhile the world underneath it keeps moving: prices change, services change, regulations change, platforms change their features, statistics age, and links quietly break. AI can make the job of keeping content accurate far more manageable for a small team. It can compare old content against new information, flag what looks outdated, draft the affected section, and check whether other content depends on what just changed. But AI cannot decide, on its own authority, that a law has changed, that a medical recommendation is current, or that a source is trustworthy. This article lays out a workflow that uses AI to do the scanning and drafting, while keeping a human in charge of verification and judgment.

This is a general Locatria workflow, applicable to any local business maintaining a body of content — not a dental-specific, law-specific, or real-estate-specific guide. Examples from those three industries appear throughout because they illustrate higher-stakes content maintenance, but the workflow itself applies broadly.

## Key Takeaways

- Do not update content because it is old. Update content because something important has changed, or because it can be meaningfully improved.
- A content update, a content refresh, and a content rewrite are three different levels of intervention. Use the smallest one that actually fixes the problem.
- Published date and updated date are not proxies for quality. A recent article can be wrong; an old article can still be accurate.
- Every meaningful update should be backed by an observation, a source, a date, an impact assessment, and an action — not just "AI says this needs updating."
- AI can compare content against new information, flag possible changes, and draft affected sections. It cannot verify that a law has changed, that a medical claim is current, or that a source is authoritative — a human does that.
- When a canonical source changes, dependent content (FAQs, checklists, repurposed pieces) needs a dependency check — but not an automatic, blanket rewrite.
- Content integrity is the core discipline of this workflow: update what changed, and preserve everything that remains correct.

## 1. What Is an AI Content Update Workflow?

An AI content update workflow is a repeatable, evidence-based process for keeping already-published content accurate, current, and dependable over time. It moves from monitoring for change, through verifying that a change matters, to updating only what needs updating, checking what else depends on that content, and documenting the result.

It is different from simply asking an AI tool, every so often, "does this article need updating?" That approach produces vague, unverifiable answers. A workflow instead treats every potential update as a claim that needs evidence: what changed, where the new information came from, when it was verified, what it affects, and what action follows. AI supports several stages of this process — especially comparison, drafting, and dependency mapping — but it does not replace editorial judgment.

---

## 2. Why Evergreen Content Still Needs Maintenance

"Evergreen" describes a topic that stays relevant over time — not information that never changes. An article titled "How to Choose a Local [Service Provider]" can remain a genuinely useful, evergreen topic for years. But specific details inside that article — prices, regulations, tools, platform features, statistics, recommended steps — can and do change underneath a stable topic.

Treating a topic as evergreen and its content as permanently correct are two different assumptions, and only the first one is usually safe. A mature content system separates "is this topic still relevant?" from "is everything stated in this article still true?"

## 3. Content Update vs. Content Refresh vs. Content Rewrite

| | Content Update | Content Refresh | Content Rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Change information that is outdated, incorrect, incomplete, or materially changed | Improve content because new evidence, clearer explanations, new questions, or better structure can improve usefulness | Substantially replace the existing article or its structure |
| **Typical Trigger** | A fact, price, policy, regulation, or source has changed | The article works but could be more useful, clearer, or more complete | The topic has changed so much, or the original approach was flawed enough, that revision isn't sufficient |
| **Scope** | One or a few sections | Multiple sections, often including structure or examples | The whole article |
| **Effort** | Low to moderate | Moderate | High |
| **Risk** | Low, if scoped correctly | Low to moderate | Higher — more can go wrong across a larger surface area |
| **Example** | Updating a changed phone number or a superseded statistic | Adding a clearer explanation, a table, or a new FAQ based on recurring customer questions | Replacing an outdated regulatory guide after a major legal change reshapes the entire topic |

Do not rewrite an entire article when only one section is outdated. Prefer a localized update over an unnecessary full rewrite. A changed business hour needs its hours updated — not a new introduction, a new structure, and a rewritten conclusion.

---

## 4. Content Freshness vs. Content Accuracy

Published date does not equal content freshness, and updated date does not equal content quality. A page can carry a recent "last updated" stamp and still contain an inaccurate claim if the date was changed without a meaningful edit behind it. Conversely, an article published years ago can still be entirely correct if nothing it describes has actually changed.

Google's own guidance to creators makes a similar point: content is expected to stay current, but the standard is usefulness and reliability, not simply recency — its people-first content guidance asks creators whether a page still gives visitors a satisfying, trustworthy experience, not merely whether the date on it is recent. Do not change a publication date without a meaningful underlying change; using a date as a manufactured freshness signal is a form of the "deceptive freshness" pattern that undermines reader trust and that search systems are increasingly built to recognize. Focus instead on five things together: accuracy, relevance, usefulness, source validity, and current context.

## 5. When Should Local Businesses Update Content?

Not every content asset needs the same update frequency. Practical triggers fall into several categories:
- **Business** — a new service, a discontinued service, a new location, a closed location, a changed address or phone number, changed business hours.
- **Content** — a recurring customer question the article doesn't yet answer, a gap identified during an audit.
- **Customer** — patterns from review responses that reveal recurring confusion or a missing explanation.
- **Technology** — a tool, platform, or software referenced in the article has changed how it works.
- **Regulatory** — a law, rule, or professional guideline referenced in the article has changed.
- **Platform** — a review platform, booking system, or advertising platform has updated its policies or features.
- **Source** — the original source cited in the article has been updated, retracted, or replaced.
- **Performance** — a content audit shows a page underperforming in a way traceable to outdated information.

## 7. Establish a Canonical Source

For every important claim in a piece of content, there should be a clear answer to the question: what is the source of truth? Canonical sources typically include:
- Official business information (the business's own records, policies, hours, and offerings)
- Official government sources
- Authoritative professional organizations relevant to the industry
- Primary research
- Official platform documentation (for claims about how a platform works)

Do not treat competitor content as the default source of truth. A competitor's article may itself be outdated, incomplete, or simply wrong — using it as a reference point risks propagating someone else's error rather than verifying the underlying fact.

---

## 8. Monitor Content Change Signals

A business doesn't need to re-verify every article every week. Instead, monitor for signals that something may have changed:
- Internal business changes (new services, closed locations, policy changes)
- Changes at the source (a government page updates, a platform changes its documentation)
- Recurring customer questions that suggest a gap
- Recurring themes in review responses
- Website changes elsewhere on the business's own site that may conflict with older content
- Platform updates (search platforms, review platforms, booking systems)
- New authoritative research relevant to the topic
- Broken links discovered during routine checks

AI can help organize and summarize these signals — for example, scanning a batch of source pages for changes, or summarizing a set of recent reviews for recurring questions — but a human decides which signals warrant action.

---

## 9. Content Change Detection

Change detection is the process of comparing current content against current sources to see whether anything material has shifted. Practical methods include:
- Manual review by a content owner
- Scheduled source monitoring (checking known canonical sources on a regular cadence)
- AI-assisted comparison of the article's claims against updated source material
- Change logs maintained by external sources (many government and platform sites publish their own changelogs)
- Periodic website audits
- Customer feedback and recurring questions

AI should identify possible differences between the article and its sources. A human then confirms whether the difference actually matters — not every wording change in a source represents a material change to the underlying fact.

---

## 10. Content Update Impact Assessment

For every detected change, work through these questions before touching the article:
1. What changed?
2. Is it verified against an authoritative source?
3. Does it actually affect this article?
4. Which specific section is affected?
5. Does it affect other content (dependencies)?
6. Is the change high risk (privacy, medical, legal, financial, safety)?
7. Is a minor update enough to address it?
8. Does the article need a full rewrite instead?
9. Does the cited source itself need updating or replacing?
10. Does the publication or "last updated" date need to change as a result?

---

## 11. Update Scope Decision Framework

Once impact is assessed, decide the scope of intervention:

```
NO CHANGE → MINOR UPDATE → SECTION UPDATE → MAJOR UPDATE → FULL REWRITE → ARCHIVE
```

- **No Change** — the content is still accurate; document that it was reviewed.
- **Minor Update** — a small factual correction (a phone number, a date, a statistic) with no structural impact.
- **Section Update** — one or more sections need revision, but the article's overall structure and remaining content stay intact.
- **Major Update** — several sections are affected, and the update meaningfully changes a significant portion of the article's guidance.
- **Full Rewrite** — the topic or the article's original approach has changed enough that revision is no longer the right tool.
- **Archive** — the content is no longer relevant or accurate enough to keep live, and should be removed or clearly marked as outdated rather than updated.

---

## 12. Source Verification

Before making any update, verify the new information against an appropriate authoritative source:
- Official government sources for regulatory or legal information
- Professional organizations for industry-standard guidance
- Primary research for statistics and data claims
- The business's own current, verified information for anything about the business itself

Do not use AI output as the final source. An AI tool can point toward where a change may exist and can help summarize a source once it's been retrieved, but the authoritative source itself — not the AI's restatement of it — is what should be cited and relied upon.

---

## 13. AI-Assisted Content Comparison

AI is well suited to comparing an existing section of content against a piece of verified new source material and categorizing the relationship:
- **Unchanged** — the existing claim still matches the source.
- **Changed** — the source clearly contradicts the existing claim.
- **Potentially Outdated** — the existing claim may no longer be accurate, but the comparison isn't conclusive.
- **Unsupported** — the existing claim isn't backed by any available source, old or new.
- **Requires Verification** — the comparison surfaced enough uncertainty that a human needs to check the primary source directly.

AI should preserve uncertainty rather than resolve it artificially. A confident-sounding "this is now outdated" from an AI tool is not the same thing as a verified fact.

---

## 14. Update Drafting

Once a change is verified, AI can help draft only the affected section — not the whole article. Provide:
- The original section, verbatim
- The verified new information
- The source for that new information
- Specific update instructions (what should change, what should stay the same)

The output should be a revised version of that specific section. Do not ask AI to rewrite the entire article unless the assessed scope in Section 11 actually calls for a major update or full rewrite.

---

## 15. Content Integrity During Updates

This is the most important discipline in the entire workflow. When updating content, do not:
- Remove accurate content unnecessarily
- Remove important context
- Remove qualifications ("in most cases," "this varies by," "consult a professional for your specific situation")
- Remove source information
- Remove examples without a specific reason
- Remove FAQ items without individual review
- Remove checklist items without individual review
- Change the meaning of sections that are still accurate
- Introduce unsupported claims in the process of "improving" the wording

The updated content should preserve everything that remains valid. The core rule of this entire workflow is simple: **update what changed, preserve what remains correct.**

---

## 16. Human Editorial Review

Every update — regardless of how small — passes through a human reviewer before publishing. Check:
- Factual accuracy of the new information
- Quality and authority of the cited source
- Whether the date fields (published, last updated, last reviewed) are correctly reflected
- Whether surrounding context still makes sense
- Whether qualifications were preserved
- Tone consistency with the rest of the article
- Internal links (do they still point somewhere accurate?)
- External links (are they still live and still support the claim?)
- Examples (are they still relevant, or did the update make one stale?)
- Tables (do the numbers and labels still align?)
- FAQs (are all items still accurate and complete?)
- Prompts, if the article contains reusable AI prompts
- Screenshots or visual references (do they still match the current interface or process being described?)
- Any other structured information (checklists, decision trees, workflows)

## 17. High-Risk Content Updates

Some categories of content carry higher stakes when updated, and deserve dedicated care.

**Dental:** Medical claims, treatment information, clinical guidance, and any patient-facing claims should be reviewed by someone qualified to assess clinical accuracy before publishing an update. AI can flag that a claim may be outdated; it cannot confirm that a clinical recommendation is current.

**Law:** Laws, regulations, deadlines, legal procedures, and statements about legal rights require verification against an authoritative legal or government source, ideally with review by someone qualified to interpret the change. AI can identify that a regulation may have changed; it cannot confirm the interpretation or the effective date on its own authority.

**Real Estate:** Market data, financing information, tax-related statements, legal requirements, investment-related claims, and Fair Housing–sensitive content all require careful verification. AI-assisted updates to real estate content should be checked against current, authoritative sources, and language should be reviewed for anything that could read as discriminatory or steering-related under the Fair Housing Act.

Across all three: AI can identify that something may have changed. Qualified humans should verify high-risk information before it goes live. None of this workflow's guidance should be read as, or should produce, individualized medical, legal, financial, tax, mortgage, or investment advice — it is a process for maintaining educational and informational content responsibly.

## 18. Dependency Check

Content rarely exists in isolation. After updating a canonical source, check whether any of the following depend on it:
- FAQs derived from the same information
- Checklists built on the same guidance
- Related articles that reference the same facts
- Repurposed content (social posts, newsletters, video scripts) drawn from the canonical piece
- Internal training materials
- Screenshots or diagrams that illustrate the same process
- Any other resource that cites or restates the updated information

This is the concept of dependent content: pieces that inherit accuracy (or inaccuracy) from a canonical source. A change at the source doesn't automatically mean every dependent piece needs the identical change, but every dependent piece deserves a review to find out.

---

## 19. Update Propagation Workflow

When a canonical source changes, follow this sequence rather than editing everything at once:

```
SOURCE CHANGES → IDENTIFY DEPENDENCIES → ASSESS IMPACT → UPDATE CANONICAL CONTENT → UPDATE DEPENDENT CONTENT → QA → PUBLISH → DOCUMENT
```

Do not automatically update every dependent asset the moment the canonical source changes. Review each dependency individually — some may need the same correction, some may need no change at all, and some may need a different, smaller adjustment appropriate to their own format.

## 36. Common AI Content Update Mistakes

1. Updating content simply because it is old without change evidence
2. Changing publication dates without meaningful content updates
3. Rewriting entire articles unnecessarily when section updates suffice
4. Letting AI declare information outdated without source proof
5. Using AI output as the source of truth rather than human verification
6. Accidental content or qualification loss during edits
7. Failing to update dependent content after canonical source changes
8. Keeping broken links or outdated screenshots
9. Ignoring regulatory or business info changes
10. Optimizing for freshness dates instead of genuine accuracy

## 38. What AI Can and Cannot Do

**AI CAN:** Compare content against source material, flag potential changes, organize evidence, draft affected sections, map dependencies, and audit integrity.  
**AI CANNOT GUARANTEE:** Factual, legal, medical, or financial accuracy, source authority, search rankings, AI visibility, traffic, or leads without human verification.

## 39. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What is an AI content update workflow?

It is a repeatable process for keeping published content accurate over time — monitoring for change, verifying that a change is real and material, updating only the affected content, checking dependent content, and documenting the result.

### How often should local businesses update their content?

Set review triggers per topic rate of change rather than applying one blanket schedule to all content — fast-moving topics need frequent checks, while stable processes need less.

### Does old content automatically become outdated?

No. Age alone does not make content wrong. Accuracy is verified against sources, not inferred from a date.

### What is dependent content?

Dependent content is any additional asset (FAQ, checklist, repurposed post, newsletter) that restates or relies on information from a canonical source.
