# AI Review Response Workflow for Local Businesses

Customer reviews are one of the few places where a local business speaks directly, and publicly, to the people deciding whether to trust it. A dental clinic, a law firm, a real estate brokerage, a plumber, a bakery — all of them now live partly in the comments section of the internet. AI can help a small team keep up with that conversation. It can classify reviews, draft responses, and catch risky language before it goes live. But a review response is not a private draft. It is a public statement, often permanent, sometimes read by regulators, opposing counsel, or a patient's family. This article lays out a workflow that lets AI do the heavy lifting of drafting and organizing, while keeping a human squarely in charge of what actually gets published.

This is a general Locatria workflow. It applies to any local business — dental practices, law firms, real estate teams, restaurants, home services, retail, and beyond. Dental, legal, and real estate examples are used throughout because those industries carry extra privacy and professional-conduct risk, not because this workflow is exclusive to them.

## Key Takeaways

- A review response workflow is a repeatable process, not a single prompt typed into a chatbot each time a review appears.
- AI drafts. Humans decide. The business owns the response — legally, reputationally, and ethically.
- Sentiment and risk are two different dimensions. A five-star review can still be high-risk if it contains private information; a one-star review can be low-risk if it is a simple pricing complaint.
- Privacy protection applies even when the reviewer volunteers private details first. Public disclosure by a customer does not create permission for the business to confirm or expand on it.
- Dental, legal, and real estate businesses carry extra obligations — patient privacy, client confidentiality, and fair housing rules — that a generic AI prompt will not know to respect.
- Automatic publishing of AI-generated responses should not be the default. Human review is a required stage, not an optional one.
- Review responses feed a larger content system: recurring questions in reviews are a legitimate source of FAQ and content ideas.

## 1. What Is an AI-Assisted Review Response Workflow?

An AI-assisted review response workflow is a defined, repeatable sequence of steps a business follows every time it receives a customer review — from the moment the review appears to the moment the situation is fully closed and documented. It is not the same thing as opening a chatbot and typing "write a response to this review." That approach produces a response with no classification step, no privacy check, no context verification, and no consistent approval process. It is fast, but it is fragile.

A workflow, by contrast, treats every review as passing through the same stations: capture, classification, risk assessment, sensitive-information screening, internal context verification, response-type selection, AI drafting, human review, privacy review, approval, publishing, and — where needed — escalation and follow-up. AI participates at several of these stations. A human is accountable at all of them.

---

## 2. Why Review Responses Matter for Local Businesses

Reviews are often the first thing a prospective customer sees about a business, and the response underneath a review is often the second. A thoughtful reply can:
- Show a prospective customer how the business treats people when something goes wrong.
- Correct a factual misunderstanding without being defensive.
- Demonstrate that the business reads and acts on feedback.
- Give an unhappy customer a path to resolution outside the public thread.
- Provide a small amount of useful context for someone comparing several similar businesses.

This article does not claim that replying to reviews automatically improves search rankings, that a specific reply frequency guarantees an SEO benefit, or that any response strategy can guarantee a particular business outcome. What a good workflow can promise is consistency, professionalism, and a reduced chance of a privacy or compliance mistake — which is valuable on its own, independent of any ranking effect.

## 3. Review Response vs. Review Generation

These are two separate workflows and this article covers only the first one.

| | Review Response | Review Generation |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Replying to a review a customer has already posted | Encouraging customers to leave new reviews |
| **Direction** | Reactive — happens after the review exists | Proactive — happens before a review exists |
| **Governance** | Content policies for replies | Platform solicitation policies, plus the FTC's rule on reviews and testimonials |
| **Risk area** | Privacy, tone, accuracy, professional conduct | Incentivized reviews, review gating, fake reviews |

This article does not provide guidance on soliciting reviews. It does not encourage fake reviews, fake customers, incentivized reviews where prohibited, review gating (screening customers by sentiment before directing them to a public platform), fabricated testimonials, or asking AI to write or impersonate a customer review. Businesses that want to build a review-generation process should treat it as a distinct workflow, governed by the review platform's own policies and by the FTC's current rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, which specifically prohibits selling or buying fake reviews and prohibits conditioning incentives on the sentiment a reviewer expresses.

---

## 4. Review Response vs. Review Removal

Responding to a review and requesting its removal are different actions, aimed at different problems.

**Responding is communication.** It is written for two audiences at once: the original reviewer, and every future reader who compares the business to competitors.

**Requesting removal is a moderation request** submitted through the review platform's official process, used when a review may violate the platform's content policies — for example, when it is not based on a genuine experience, contains hate speech, or was posted by someone with no real interaction with the business. Google, for instance, allows businesses to flag reviews they believe violate its content policies, and reviewers or businesses can add supporting evidence during the platform's review window.

A negative review that is simply unflattering, or that reflects a genuine but unhappy customer experience, is not a candidate for removal — it is a candidate for a thoughtful response. Do not recommend retaliation against reviewers. Do not recommend threatening a reviewer to get a review taken down; the FTC's current rule specifically prohibits using unfounded legal threats, intimidation, or false accusations to suppress a negative review. Do not encourage removing legitimate negative feedback simply because it is negative — Google's own guidance to businesses is that a mix of positive and negative reviews reads as more trustworthy to consumers than an unnaturally perfect record.

## 5. Review Classification Framework

Every incoming review should be classified before anyone — human or AI — drafts a response. A single review can belong to more than one category at once; for example, a review can be both a "Service Complaint" and a "Sensitive Review" if it also references a medical detail.

| Review Type | Typical Signal | Risk | Response Approach | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | Praise, thanks, high rating | Usually low, but check for embedded private details | Thank, acknowledge specifics where appropriate | Rarely |
| Neutral | Factual, mixed tone, no strong sentiment | Low to medium | Answer the question or observation directly | Occasionally |
| Negative | Complaint, low rating, frustration | Medium to high depending on content | Acknowledge, verify context, offer next step | Sometimes |
| Mixed | Both praise and criticism in one review | Medium | Address both parts individually | Occasionally |
| Question-Based | Reviewer is asking something publicly | Low to medium | Answer factually; avoid confirming private details | Rarely |
| Service Complaint | General dissatisfaction with process or experience | Medium | Acknowledge, verify, offer resolution path | Sometimes |
| Staff Complaint | Names or describes an employee | Medium to high | Avoid confirming names publicly; escalate internally | Often |
| Pricing Complaint | Disputes cost or billing | Medium | Clarify general pricing policy without disclosing account details | Occasionally |
| Delay/Scheduling Complaint | References wait times or missed appointments | Low to medium | Acknowledge, offer to follow up privately | Occasionally |
| Quality Complaint | Product or service did not meet expectations | Medium | Acknowledge, verify, offer resolution | Occasionally |
| Sensitive Review | References medical, legal, financial, or safety details | High | Minimal public response; escalate | Almost always |
| Potentially Fraudulent/Irrelevant | Content does not match the business or seems unrelated | Medium | Internal verification before any public reply | Often |
| Requiring Escalation | Threats, harassment, safety concerns, potential litigation | High | Do not respond publicly without appropriate review | Always |

## 6. Sentiment vs. Risk Framework

This distinction is central to the entire workflow: **sentiment is not the same thing as risk.**

**Sentiment** describes how the reviewer feels — positive, negative, neutral, or mixed. **Risk** describes how much care the response requires — how much potential for privacy exposure, legal exposure, or reputational harm exists in either the review or the response.

- A five-star review that says "Dr. [Name] was amazing during my treatment for [condition]" is positive in sentiment but high-risk, because confirming or elaborating on it in a public reply could disclose protected health information.
- A one-star review that says "the wait was too long" is negative in sentiment but low-risk, because it involves no private information and no professional-conduct concern.
- A neutral-sounding review that mentions a dispute over a legal fee may require escalation even though its tone is calm.

Treat sentiment and risk as two separate fields in your classification process, not one combined score.

## 7. Review Intake Process

Capture only what is operationally necessary. At intake, record:
- The review text, as posted
- The platform it came from (Google, Yelp, Facebook, industry-specific platforms, etc.)
- The date and time posted
- The star rating, if applicable
- The business location, for multi-location businesses
- Reviewer information only as necessary — typically just the public display name, not additional personal details
- An internal reference ID for tracking
- The current response status

Do not collect more personal information about the reviewer than the platform already makes public. There is no need to look up a reviewer's identity, contact details, or history beyond what is required to verify legitimate context internally.

---

## 8. Sensitive Information Detection

Before drafting anything, scan the review itself for signals of sensitive content, including:
- Full names (the reviewer's or a third party's)
- Contact details (phone numbers, email addresses)
- Medical information (diagnoses, treatments, conditions, medications)
- Legal information (case details, opposing party names, legal strategy)
- Financial information (amounts paid, account details, loan terms)
- Transaction details (property addresses, closing dates, specific offers)
- Appointment details (dates, times, specific services rendered)
- Private communications quoted or paraphrased from texts, emails, or calls

AI can flag potential sensitive information efficiently — it is well-suited to pattern recognition across large volumes of text. But AI flags should always be verified by a human before a response is drafted or published, because the cost of missing a genuine privacy issue is high and the cost of a human double-check is low.

---

## 9. Context Verification

AI does not know what actually happened between the business and the customer. It only knows what is written in the review. Before drafting any response to a complaint, verify the internal facts using sources the business is already authorized to access, such as:
- Service records
- Appointment or scheduling systems
- Customer service notes
- Transaction records
- Staff reports of the interaction

Use only information the business is authorized to access for this purpose, and never include confidential internal details in the public-facing response — internal verification informs the response; it does not become the content of the response.

---

## 10. Response Type Framework

Match the response to the situation rather than defaulting to one template for everything:
- **Thank You** — simple acknowledgment of positive feedback.
- **Acknowledgement** — recognizes the review without extensive elaboration.
- **Clarification** — corrects a factual misunderstanding without confirming private details.
- **Apology / Regret** — expresses genuine regret for a bad experience, without admitting fault the business has not verified.
- **Service Recovery** — offers a concrete next step to resolve the issue.
- **Offline Follow-Up** — invites the reviewer to continue the conversation through a private channel.
- **Escalation** — the situation is routed internally before any public response is considered.
- **No Public Response / Moderation Path** — the review is reported through the platform's official process instead of being answered publicly.

---

## 11. AI-Assisted Drafting

Give the AI drafting step a narrow, well-defined set of inputs rather than an open-ended request:
- The review text
- The business's response policy
- The desired tone
- Relevant, verified business context
- A list of facts the response is allowed to state
- A list of disclosures that are prohibited
- The selected response type

The output should be a single draft response — not a final, publish-ready statement. AI should not be allowed to invent missing facts to fill gaps in the story; where information is uncertain, the draft should say so or leave it out, and a human fills the gap from verified internal context.

---

## 12. Human Review

Every AI-drafted response passes through a human reviewer before it goes further. The reviewer checks for:
- **Accuracy** — does the draft match verified internal facts?
- **Privacy** — does it avoid disclosing anything it shouldn't?
- **Tone** — does it match the business's voice and stay non-defensive?
- **Promises** — does it commit the business to anything it cannot guarantee?
- **Facts** — are all factual claims verified, not assumed?
- **Professional boundaries** — does it stay within what's appropriate for the industry?
- **Escalation** — should this go to someone else before publishing?
- **Platform appropriateness** — does it fit the platform's content policies?
- **Brand consistency** — does it sound like the business?

---

## 13. Privacy Review

Treat privacy review as its own dedicated gate, separate from general human review, and ask explicitly:
- Does this confirm the reviewer's identity as a customer, patient, or client?
- Does this reveal private information about the reviewer?
- Does this repeat sensitive information the reviewer included in their review?
- Does this disclose internal records or notes?
- Does this expose another person's information?
- Does this reveal legal, medical, or financial details?

If the answer to any of these is yes, the response is revised or escalated — it does not move forward as written. This applies even when the reviewer disclosed the sensitive detail publicly first. A customer's own public disclosure does not authorize the business to confirm, elaborate on, or repeat that same information.

---

## 14. Approval Framework

Approval requirements should scale with risk level, not apply uniformly to every review:
- **Low risk** — routine positive or neutral feedback. Trained staff may approve according to the business's written policy.
- **Medium risk** — complaints, disputes, unclear facts, pricing concerns, service issues. A manager reviews before publishing.
- **High risk** — privacy, medical, legal, safety, threats, or harassment concerns. Requires review appropriate to the situation — which may include a privacy officer, legal counsel, or a clinical lead, depending on the business and the issue.

This article does not prescribe universal legal requirements for who must approve what. Each business should establish governance appropriate to its own industry obligations, size, and risk tolerance.

---

## 15. Publishing Workflow

Before a response goes live, confirm:
- The response has completed its required review stage(s)
- The correct platform is selected
- The final wording matches what was approved
- The date of publication is recorded
- The responder (person or role) is recorded
- The response status is updated in the tracking system

Automated publishing should not be the default behavior of an AI-assisted workflow. A human takes the final action to publish.

---

## 16. Escalation Framework

Not every review should be resolved by the first person who sees it. Route reviews to the appropriate internal path:
- **Customer Service** — general service issues.
- **Manager** — disputes, moderate complaints, staff mentions.
- **Privacy** — any review touching personal, medical, legal, or financial information.
- **Legal** — potential litigation, defamation concerns, contractual disputes.
- **Medical / Clinical** — anything involving a diagnosis, treatment, or clinical outcome.
- **Safety** — threats, harassment, or anything suggesting a safety concern.
- **Platform Policy** — reviews that may violate the platform's own content rules.
- **Potential Fraud** — reviews that appear to be from someone with no genuine relationship to the business.
- **Threat / Harassment** — reviews containing intimidation directed at staff or the business.

Not every issue should be solved publicly. Some are resolved through a phone call, a private message, or an internal correction — with no further public statement needed beyond the initial acknowledgment.

---

## 17. Follow-Up Framework

Publishing a response is not necessarily the end of the process:

```
Response Published → Monitor Engagement → Internal Action → Document Outcome → Close Item
```

If the underlying issue points to a real service gap — a recurring scheduling problem, a training gap, a process breakdown — that internal action matters more than the public reply itself.

## 21. Response Tone Framework

Aim for: **warm, professional, calm, respectful, specific, human.**  
Avoid: **defensive, sarcastic, aggressive, overly promotional, robotic, dismissive.**

Consistency in a review response program should come from shared principles and a shared workflow — not from every response using identical wording.

## 22. Positive Review Workflow

```
Positive Review → Check Sensitive Info → Thank Reviewer → Acknowledge Specifics → Avoid Over-Promotion → Human Review → Publish
```

Even a glowing review can contain something that shouldn't be repeated publicly (treatment details, case outcomes, transaction amounts). The privacy check applies regardless of sentiment.

---

## 23. Neutral Review Workflow

```
Neutral Review → Understand Context → Assess Value → Answer Question/Observation → Human Review → Publish
```

Not every neutral review requires a reply. If a response would add nothing beyond a generic acknowledgment, a brief, genuine one is still often worthwhile.

---

## 24. Negative Review Workflow

```
Negative Review → Classify Complaint → Verify Context Internally → Risk Check → AI Draft → Human Review → Privacy Review → Publish → Offline Follow-Up
```

Do not argue publicly. A negative review response is written as much for the next reader as for the original reviewer.

---

## 25. Sensitive Review Workflow

```
Sensitive Review → Identify Sensitivity → Exclude Private Details → Escalate Internally → Draft Minimal Response → Approval → Publish / Private Channel Link
```

For high-risk categories (medical, legal, financial, safety), the safest public response is often short, neutral, and non-identifying, paired with an invitation to continue privately.

---

## 26. Potentially Fraudulent or Irrelevant Review Workflow

```
Review → Internal Verification → Platform Policy Check → Appropriate Response or Moderation Flag → Document
```

Do not publicly accuse a reviewer of fraud or dishonesty without evidence. If internal records show no relationship, report the review through the platform's official moderation process.

## 27. AI Prompt — Review Classifier

```
You are assisting a local business in classifying an incoming customer review. You are not deciding how to respond. You are only classifying.

INPUT:
- Review text: [insert]
- Business context: [insert]
- Response policy summary: [insert]

Analyze the review and output:
- Review Type (one or more from the standard classification list)
- Sentiment (positive, negative, neutral, mixed)
- Risk (low, medium, high) — assessed independently of sentiment
- Sensitive Information Flag (yes/no, with brief explanation)
- Potential Escalation Category (if any)
- Suggested Response Type

Rules:
- If evidence is insufficient to classify any field with confidence, output "UNKNOWN" for that field rather than guessing.
- Do not state or imply that a review is fraudulent without clear evidence in the review itself.
- Do not infer facts about the business's conduct beyond what the review states.
```

## 28. AI Prompt — Review Response Drafting

```
You are drafting one response to a customer review on behalf of a local business. You are producing a draft for human review, not a final public statement.

INPUT:
- Review: [insert]
- Business name and basic context: [insert]
- Selected Response Type: [insert]
- Approved facts (verified internally, explicitly provided): [insert]
- Tone policy: [insert]
- Privacy rules: [insert]

Produce exactly one draft response.

Rules:
- Do not invent facts not provided to you.
- Do not confirm the reviewer's identity as a customer, patient, or client.
- Do not mention confidential records, internal notes, or case/appointment details.
- Do not make promises the business has not authorized.
- Do not argue with the reviewer or dispute their account of events.
- Do not accuse the reviewer of dishonesty.
- Do not provide individualized medical, legal, or financial/tax/investment advice.
- Do not attempt to persuade the reviewer to change or remove their review.
- Do not use excessive marketing or promotional language.
```

## 29. AI Prompt — Privacy and Risk Auditor

```
You are auditing a draft review response before it is considered for publication.

INPUT:
- Original review: [insert]
- Draft response: [insert]

Audit the draft response for:
- Personal information about the reviewer or any third party
- Medical information
- Legal information
- Financial information
- Transaction details
- Confirmation of identity (as a customer, patient, or client)
- Unsupported claims
- Promises the business may not be able to keep
- Risky, defensive, or inflammatory language
- Anything that should trigger escalation

Output one of: PASS, PARTIAL, FAIL — with a brief explanation for the result. A FAIL or PARTIAL result means the draft should not be published as written.
```

## 30. AI Prompt — Response Quality Auditor

```
You are evaluating the quality of a draft review response, separate from its privacy risk.

- Original review: [insert]

- Business's tone and brand guidelines: [insert]

Evaluate on:
- Relevance to the review
- Accuracy against provided facts
- Tone
- Empathy
- Privacy (flag only; full audit is separate)
- Clarity
- Specificity
- Professionalism
- Whether escalation appears warranted
- Brand consistency

Do not judge quality based on whether specific marketing keywords are present. Provide a short assessment and, if useful, one or two specific suggestions.
```

## 31. AI Prompt — Response Consistency Auditor

```
You are checking a set of already-approved review responses against the business's written response policy.

INPUT:
- Business's response policy: [insert]
- Set of previously approved responses: [insert]

Check whether responses consistently follow:
- Tone guidelines
- Privacy rules
- Escalation practices
- General response structure
- Prohibited language
- Professional boundaries appropriate to the industry

Do not flag responses simply because they use different wording from one another. Consistency means consistent principles, not identical wording. Output any patterns of drift from the stated policy.
```

## 39. Common AI Review Response Mistakes

1. Automatically publishing AI responses without human review
2. Confirming customer identity in a public reply
3. Repeating sensitive information the reviewer included
4. Arguing with reviewers in the public thread
5. Accusing reviewers of lying without evidence
6. Inventing facts the AI wasn't given
7. Making unsupported promises the business can't guarantee
8. Using the same robotic response on every review
9. Over-promoting the business inside a reply meant to address a concern
10. Ignoring negative feedback entirely

## 41. What AI Can and Cannot Do

**AI CAN:** Classify reviews, identify topics, flag potential privacy risks, draft responses, audit tone, audit privacy compliance, and organize review data.  
**AI CANNOT GUARANTEE:** Customer satisfaction, reputation improvement, review removal, search rankings, AI visibility, leads, or legal/medical correctness without human verification.

## 42. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### What is an AI review response workflow?

An AI review response workflow is a repeatable, staged process for handling customer reviews — from capture and classification through AI drafting, human review, privacy review, approval, publishing, and follow-up.

### Can AI automatically respond to customer reviews?

AI can draft responses, but automatic publishing should not be the default. A human should review and approve responses before publication, especially for anything beyond routine positive feedback.

### How should dental clinics protect patient privacy in review responses?

Avoid confirming that the reviewer is a patient, avoid confirming or discussing any treatment or medical history, and avoid discussing payment or appointment details publicly.

### How should law firms handle reviews involving confidential matters?

Avoid confirming or denying an attorney-client relationship, avoid discussing case details or legal strategy, and avoid drawing public legal conclusions.
